# Harry's speech comparison: how to read the two transcripts and write the fixes

## The idea
You sound like a foreigner because of a handful of details natives do differently: how long your sentences run and how you chain them, the little words between ideas, how you end sentences, how you soften things, the fillers and responses you use. A native speaker you copy (your language parent) already does all of it. So you compare yourself **against them**, find the few biggest, easiest differences, and fix those. Harry did this with Claude against a speaker he wanted to sound like, and his speaking jumped within a month.

## The rules
- **Compare against the speaker, never against your own idea of good Japanese.** Every fix must point to something the speaker actually says, quoted from their transcript with its file and time ([02 4:31]). If you can't find it in their transcript, it isn't a fix.
- **Only the 5-10 easiest fixes, not every difference.** Easy means: small to change, comes up a lot, and makes a big difference to how natural you sound. A long list nobody acts on is worse than five fixes they use tomorrow.
- **Quote the learner too.** Each fix shows one or two of the learner's real lines (file and time), then the same idea said the speaker's way.
- **Use the speaker's own words and chunks** in the rewrites. Don't invent fancier Japanese the speaker never uses.
- **Text can't hear.** These transcripts show words, grammar, endings and timing, not sounds, pitch or accent. Never give a pronunciation or pitch fix from them. Say that pronunciation is a separate job: record yourself, listen next to the speaker, and practise the sounds you hear are off.
- **Whisper sometimes mishears.** If a word looks strange, check the line around it. Never build a fix on one odd line.
- **Kansai or standard is the learner's choice**, decided by their language parent. Don't "correct" a learner toward standard if their speaker mixes in Kansai, or the other way round.

## Before you start
- **Read the transcripts in full,** in pieces if they're long. Compare counts as rates a minute (stats.md gives them), never raw counts: the two recordings are different lengths.
- **Lines that aren't the learner talking** (reading comments aloud, another voice, a clip they played) get ignored. Mention it if there's a lot.
- **A short recording (under 20 minutes):** still do the comparison, say the fixes rest on less material, and ask for a longer one next time.
- **Different topics** (a product review against a vlog): only give fixes that work on any topic: endings, linking, softening, fillers, chunks. Not topic vocabulary.
- **The chunk lists are machine-made:** check every chunk in the transcript before you use it, and only report ones that are real, reusable pieces of how the speaker talks.

## What to look at (start with stats.md, then read the transcripts)
1. **Sentence length and how they chain.** Natives often run long sentences joined with て, けど, から, っていうのは. Learners stop and start: short sentences, one idea each. (Harry's own speaker used sentences 3-4 times longer than his, chained with て-form and っていうのは.)
2. **Things the learner says that the speaker never says.** Harry's example: he said した方がいい all the time; his speaker never did.
3. **How the speaker softens.** Suggestions and opinions: 悪くないんじゃないかな, かもしれない, かなって, っていう感じ. Learners are often blunter than natives.
4. **Sentence endings.** よね, んですよ, じゃないですか, かな, って感じ. Learners end too many sentences in plain です/ます.
5. **Fillers and responses.** What the speaker says while thinking (まあ, なんか, こう, えー) versus the learner's silence or English-style fillers.
6. **Chunks.** The speaker's go-to pieces (みたいな, っていう, というわけで, どっちかというと) that the learner never uses.
7. **Politeness level.** Is the learner far more or less polite than the speaker, for the same kind of talk?
8. **Speed.** Never a fix on its own: speed comes from everything else. Don't tell someone to talk faster or slower; mention a big gap only to explain it.

## The report (write it to compare/<me>-vs-<speaker>/report.md, then summarise it in the chat)
1. **What makes [speaker] sound like [speaker]:** 4-6 bullets, each with a quoted line.
2. **The fixes (5-10), easiest and most common first.** For each:
   - **What they do:** one sentence, with 1-2 quoted lines from the speaker (file and time).
   - **What you do:** one sentence, with 1-2 quoted lines from the learner (file and time).
   - **Say it their way:** one of the learner's lines rewritten the speaker's way, using the speaker's own words and chunks.
   - **Practise it:** one concrete thing: rep the chunk in your head today, use it in your next recording, and so on.
3. **What this can't tell you:** sounds, pitch and accent need your ears (one line).
4. **Next step:** practise these for 1-2 weeks, record yourself again the same way, and run the comparison again to see what's changed.

## The speaker profile (write it to <speaker>/profile.md)
The first time a speaker is analysed, save what sets them apart, so next time it can be read first and a new recording compared straight away. Use this layout:
- **Who:** name, the videos used (titles), minutes, speed a minute, casual or polite, standard or Kansai.
- **What makes them sound like them:** 4-6 bullets, each with a quoted line (file and time).
- **Their go-to endings, fillers, linking words and chunks:** each with a rate a minute and one quoted line.
- **Things they never say** that learners often do (only if the transcripts show it clearly).

## Recording yourself (tell the learner this if they haven't recorded yet)
- **Talk to a camera or your phone, alone, for 20-40 minutes.** Unedited, no script, no notes. Talking alone forces you to say things you'd never get to in a conversation.
- **Talk about the kind of things your speaker talks about**, so the comparison is fair: their topics, their kind of video.
- **Don't fix anything while recording.** The point is to capture how you really talk.
- **The speaker's side:** 3 or more videos of them talking alone, 30+ minutes in total. Group videos mix voices together, so skip them, or use --from/--to to keep only a part where they talk alone.
