# Listening and immersion

## Immersion time is listening time
- **All the time you deliberately set aside for immersion is listening.** If you'd normally read a book to relax, listen to Japanese instead.
- **Why listening:**
  - It trains the whole sound system: rhythm, timing, the little pauses.
  - It builds the mental model you'll later speak from.
  - Anki already gives you plenty of reading, so you won't fall behind on reading or kanji.

## The three kinds of listening
1. **Passive:** every second you can reasonably have earphones in (commute, dishes, gym, walking, scrolling). If you'd be listening to anything, make it Japanese. Put the video you're currently working on (below) on repeat here.
2. **The one-video grind (active):** one short video from your main creator, learned completely. Watch it properly at least once a day.
3. **Enjoyable active:** sit-down watching for fun, with full attention.
   - Go through content freely, and switch the moment you get bored.
   - The point is enjoying Japanese and keeping your attention locked in.
   - Repetition lives in passive time; active time is the grind video plus new, enjoyable content.

**Time:** no minimum. About 2 hours of active a day is ideal, and at least an hour in 15-30 minute blocks is doable for busy people. Passive goes on top, as much as you can.

**Can't focus for long?** That's normal. Nobody does hours of intense active listening in one go.
- Use more stimulating, visual content.
- Switch videos the moment your focus slips.
- Don't force active listening past your concentration.

**Passive listening:** every second you can reasonably have earphones in. It fills every gap on top of your active time, however much active time you get.

## Day one
- **Active listening starts on day one,** even though you'll understand almost nothing. That's normal and it's still working.
- **Before about 500 words:** a Japanese-only Instagram is the easiest way in. Make a fresh account, follow only Japanese creators, and scroll it for 30-60 minutes a day.

## What counts as immersion
- **Native, unscripted Japanese made for Japanese people.**
  - **Learner content** (learner podcasts, slow Japanese) feels good, but it's false progress. Beginners use a Japanese-only Instagram instead.
  - **Anime, dramas and other scripted shows are not a niche.** It's like learning English from SpongeBob: the speech is off the rails and full of mannerisms real people don't use. Watch them for fun if you like, but don't build your Japanese on them.
  - **Anything with English subtitles is leisure.** Enjoy it, but it doesn't count as study or immersion in any way, so don't log it in the JIL Tracker.
- **YouTube is the best source:** unscripted chatting, the closest thing to how people really talk, joke and react. Podcasts and talk channels are second.
- **Quick test:** think of the English equivalent. How close is it to how you and your mates actually talk? Reality shows and unscripted chat are great; scripted shows less so.

## Picking your niche and main creator
- **When:** start your niche once the base is done (Hikaru 500, one read of Tae Kim, kanji deck if you needed it).
- **Why one creator first:** it shrinks Japanese down to a manageable size. One person uses a limited set of words again and again, so you understand them fast. Then you widen.
- **Who:** someone unscripted you like the sound of, with plenty of content: at least 50 videos, ideally over 100 (50 to 100 is borderline). Hikaru is the fallback if you're unsure.
- **Main creator vs language parent:**
  - Your **main creator** is who you build your understanding on (Stage 2).
  - Your **language parent** is who you copy to sound like (Stage 3).
  - They're often the same person, and it works best if they are.
  - **You become what you listen to,** so pick someone who sounds how you want to sound, including gender.
  - **If nobody fits perfectly,** compromise on topic or age, not gender. A same-age YouTuber in one area is enough.
  - **Finding people:** use the **JIL Creator Finder** from the classroom: fill in the About You doc, paste it into the red line in the Creator Finder prompt, and run it in Claude Code. It searches YouTube for real channels that fit you, checks each one has enough videos and Japanese subtitles, and measures how fast they talk. Without Claude Code, paste the prompt into a Claude chat with web search and check the video counts yourself. Or ask Claude for Japanese YouTubers in your interests and demographic, or search your interests the way you would in English and follow the recommendations down the rabbit hole.
- **X / Twitter:** your creator's X counts as part of your niche. General X browsing doesn't.
- **Hikaru's speed:** it depends on the video. He's naturally fast, not forced fast.

## The one-video method
1. **Pick a video of about 15 minutes** from your main creator. 15-20 is the max; 10 is a bit short. It doesn't need to be exact. Talk-heavy, lightly edited videos with clear audio work best.
2. **Learn every word and chunk in it** in Anki, by making cards from its transcript with Claude (see `anki.md`).
3. **Go through it sentence by sentence.** Read each sentence and replay its audio over and over until your ear gets used to it, then move to the next, through the whole video. The **JIL sentence breakdown** skill (in Claude Code) builds a page for exactly this: play or loop each line, furigana, English, hover meanings. The Toolkit lesson shows how to install it.
4. **Watch the whole video** and check whether you understand every second.
5. **If you don't yet, keep it on passive repeat** until you're genuinely close to 100%.
6. **Move on** to the next video from the same creator. Work through their channel, then widen to other creators.

If a sentence in the video is something you'd actually want to say, it's worth being able to say it yourself.

**No passive time?** Then do step 5 as active rewatches until you understand close to 100%.

## Subtitles
- **Don't stress about them.** There's no percentage rule and no taping over the screen.
- **Most Japanese YouTube has subtitles burned in.** Try not to read them, because reading takes over from listening. You'll glance sometimes, and that's fine.
- **Early on, glancing actually helps:** you see the short common structures and chunks, so you know what to listen for.
- **The subtitle test:** pause a subtitled video and look at the words.
  - **Lots you don't know?** It's a words problem. Learn words.
  - **You know every word on screen but your ears missed them?** It's an ear problem. Pause or cut back new cards for a while and pour the time into listening.

## Looking words up
- **Don't look up every unknown word. Only look up the ones that pop out.**
  - After enough listening, you'll keep hearing the same sound until one day it stands out from the "sea of not understanding": "I've heard that before, what does it mean?"
  - Look it up and write it down to add to Anki. Your brain already knows the sound and just needs the meaning, so it's an instant learn, and you'll notice it every time after that.
  - This is one of the best payoffs of constant immersion.
- **Advanced exception:** if you understand a whole sentence except one word, look that one up too.
- **Otherwise:** look up nothing unless it pops out. Don't pause your listening to dictionary-hunt.

## "I understand almost nothing": is that normal?
- **Yes.** Early not-understanding is normal and always there to some extent.
- Comprehension doesn't grow smoothly. It comes in chunks and jumps, with flat bits in between. Keep the hours up through the flat bits.
- Don't chase every nuance; tolerate the ambiguity and keep going.

## Levelling up
- **Once your main creator is easy:** move to better and more complex speakers, podcasts and lectures. Listening to better Japanese speakers is what made Harry's own speech more articulate.
- **For podcasts:** Japanese podcasts and YouTube talk channels. Some radio shows make good podcasts, but they're hard to get hold of.
- **At an advanced level:** add dense non-fiction reading for the rare, bookish words and written grammar you won't meet in speech.
- **Real conversation is perfect immersion too.** It's 100% understandable because the other person adjusts to you, and you're fully concentrated.
- **Living in Japan:** not required. Harry never lived there. What matters is turning Japanese into the language you live in, wherever you are.
