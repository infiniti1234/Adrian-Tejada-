# Foundations: principles, kana, kanji, grammar, mindset, progress

## First principles
- **Understand the why.** Harry isn't here to spoon-feed you. He gives you the thinking behind every rule so you can make the big decisions yourself.
  - The vibe: relaxed but hardcore. Low stress, flexible, consistent.
- **Rules plant seeds, your ear does the learning.** This is one of the most important ideas in the whole method.
  - Rules (grammar points, pitch-accent rules, the kanji deck) only tell you what to listen out for.
  - The actual learning is hearing real Japanese and mimicking it until it's inseparable from the word.
  - Even people with "perfect" pitch accent learned the rules first, then trained their ear and copied real speech.
- **The X factor: do everything in Japanese.**
  - Phone language, Instagram, YouTube, music, games.
  - If you'd read a book for fun, listen to Japanese instead.
  - Every part of your life you switch over is free immersion.

## Kana (hiragana and katakana)
- **How:** write them out in a notebook from a chart until you can read them, hiragana first, then katakana. That's how Harry did it.
  - No Anki deck for kana.
  - No reviews needed; writing them out sticks.
- **How long:** about 1-2 weeks.
- **Order:** finish kana before the kanji deck or the Hikaru 500. The Japanese-only Instagram starts on day one anyway, because it needs no reading.
- **Drop romaji as soon as you can read kana.** Romaji like "ka" and "ki" look like English, but Japanese speakers make those sounds differently, and romaji keeps pulling you towards English sounds.

## Kanji
- **The 300-kanji deck is optional.**
  - **Who it's for:** a true day-one beginner who doesn't come from a Chinese-language background.
  - **Who can skip it:** anyone with some Japanese already, whatever their native language.
- **What it's for:** making kanji stop looking like random squiggles. Then your word cards stick better and readings are easier to remember. You always learn words *with* their kanji.
- **The card:** kanji on the front, English on the back.
- **Pace:** 300 kanji, no deadline, but speedrun it: as many new kanji a day as you can, ideally finishing before you start the Hikaru 500. Start small (say 5 a day) and push it up every day your retention allows:

  | Retention | What to do |
  |---|---|
  | Above 75% | Add more a day |
  | 70-75% | Hold |
  | Below 70% | Slow down |
  | Below 50% | Stop adding until it recovers |

  Keep reviewing it for about a week after you start your word cards, then delete it.
- **Recognition, not writing.** Harry wrote about 2,500-3,000 kanji from memory using English-on-the-front cards (the Remembering-the-Kanji style). Reviews took hours, he failed about half of them, and he calls it one of the worst experiences of his life. Don't do it.
- **Handwriting:** an optional extra much later, for people who need it (e.g. working in Japan). Once you read easily, a writing deck (word in hiragana on the front, write the kanji) is easy, because you already know the shapes.

## Sounds
Japanese sounds are easy to roughly approximate, but they're not English sounds. You'll hear every word constantly in your listening; audio on cards is optional. Listening builds the ear that hears the difference; recording yourself and comparing closes the gap.

## Grammar
- **Read Tae Kim's guide once.** The whole thing, advanced sections included, briefly. For each point, stop when you can roughly see what it means. Don't drill it, don't memorise it, and don't reread it over and over. The point is to recognise grammar when you hear it. Studied grammar before (a textbook, an app)? Skim it fast for anything you haven't met.
- **Confusing sentences:**
  - **Normally:** don't chase every nuance. Early on, not fully getting things is normal and always there.
  - **The one time to dig in with Claude:** you know every word and the grammar, but still can't see how the sentence means what it means. Then ask why it means that, what makes it mean that, and what each piece is doing.
- **The tricky stuff** (long describing clauses before a noun, working out who does what to whom, causatives like させる/させられる):
  1. Read that Tae Kim section once so you know what to listen for.
  2. Let immersion do the rest.
  3. Pick the patterns up as chunks from your videos, not drills.
- **Word order:** don't overthink it when speaking. Japanese lets you put things in almost any order. The exception is long describing clauses before a noun, where you have to flip the order you think in.
- **Verb pairs like 開ける/開く or 始める/始まる:** pick them up from listening. Don't learn them as pairs.

## Mindset
- **It isn't talent.** Harry's ear for copying accents may have helped his pronunciation, but his level came from the method: copying natural sources, volume and feedback.
- **The hours come from gaps, not grinding all day.** Fit Japanese into breaks, commutes and chores. That's how busy people do it.
- **Progress is invisible for long stretches,** then comes in jumps. Keep the hours up through the flat bits.
  - Small goals and tracking (the JIL Tracker) help.
  - So does a bit of healthy competitiveness.
- **Compare yourself to natives, not other learners.** That's the real target.
- **Age:** chunks and context work at any age. Younger learners just get away with more brute force.
- **Feeling like a fraud, or frustrated with mistakes:** normal at every level, including Harry's. Keep the volume up and judge yourself against where you were, not against perfect.

## How many words for what
| Words | What you can do |
|---|---|
| A few hundred | Talk one-on-one with a patient partner |
| 2,000-4,000 | Understand one YouTuber |
| 5,000-8,000 | Understand and speak within your niche |
| 8,000-10,000 | Good conversation, and most immersion makes sense |
| 10,000-20,000 | A high level |

## Past the base: finding and fixing gaps
- **After the Anki push (about 4,000-5,000 words), you learn what you meet.** Until then you keep going hard. After it, Anki slows down into short pushes when you start a new topic.
- **Target the bottleneck.** Work out whether your base (words), your mental model (listening) or your output (speaking) is holding you back, and put everything on that.
- **Holes:** immersion leaves them, like household words that never come up in your content. Skip them while building the base, then patch them on purpose for the parts of life you actually want to talk about.
- **Patching a whole topic** (work, a hobby): switch all your input to that topic, and learn its key words in a short Anki push first. Harry did this himself for business, marketing and finance.

## Stage 4 and the finish line
- **What advanced work is:** fully understanding complex topics, and closing the gaps in specialist areas.
  - Better speakers, podcasts and lectures are what raise the vocabulary you speak.
  - Dense non-fiction reading is for the rare, bookish words and written grammar that fly past in listening.
- **The finish line:**
  - native content in your niches without subtitles
  - natural conversation
  - switching to polite Japanese when it counts
- **After that:** pick a new niche, learn its words first, immerse, and keep going.
- **Optional extras for people who need them for work in Japan:**
  - business Japanese: the field's words first, then practise formal speech on purpose
  - handwriting kanji
