# Speaking, chunks, pronunciation and politeness

## Speak from day one: your head is the training ground
- **Speaking early won't ruin your accent.** Start now.
- **Talk in your head constantly.** Narrate your day, explain what you're doing, argue with yourself. That's how you find your gaps and build the ease of getting Japanese out. You'll sound bad at first; everyone does.
- **When you can't say something, ask Claude how to say it.**
  - It might be a bit off, and that's fine. The better your mental model gets, the more you'll notice when it's off.
  - Either way, it shows you which words and chunks go together, and often triggers how *you'd* say it.
  - Rep it in your head so it comes out easily in a real conversation.
- **Real conversation is perfect immersion.** It's 100% understandable because the other person brings their level down to yours, and you're fully concentrated.
  - You'll say an idea one way, they'll say it another, and you'll think "oh, that's how I should say it". That sticks forever.
- **Stretch vs safe:** practise stretch attempts in your head and with Claude. In a real conversation, confidently say the things you've practised.
- **Translating from English:** fine as a stepping stone when you're stuck. The aim is thought straight to Japanese, and that comes with input.

## Chunks: the building blocks of sounding Japanese
- **What a chunk is:** a small piece of Japanese halfway between a phrase and a piece of grammar. For example:
  - どっちかというと: if anything; I'd say more…
  - というわけで: so, with that said
  - みたいな感じで: kind of like…
  - そりゃそう: well yeah, obviously
  - ～というのは: as for…, the thing about… (学校というのは…)

  They're not quite idioms and don't translate one-for-one. They fill the gaps that basic grammar plus words can't express.
- **Why they matter:**
  - Once you want to say more than "this is this" or "I did this", chunks are what you build with.
  - Japanese is basically a few hundred chunks with the rest of your words slotted in.
  - The more chunks you know, understand and try to use, the more Japanese you sound.
- **When:** from the moment you start making your own cards, after the Hikaru 500.
- **How to collect them:** when Claude makes cards from your video, have it pull out the chunks as well as the words. They go in your deck.
- **How to practise:** use them when you talk in your head, and they'll start coming out in conversation.
- **Set phrases and proverbs:** only sparingly. Chunks beat fixed phrases.

## The two parts of output
Speaking has two separate parts:
- **ease:** getting words out at all, without freezing
- **accuracy:** getting them right

Work out which one is holding you back and work on that. You can't reproduce what you've never understood, so if your sentences stay simple, it's usually input (and chunks) you're short of. Build sentences from things you've heard before, not from translated English.

## Finding people to talk to
- **HelloTalk** is the best central place to meet lots of Japanese people for free.
- **Also:**
  - in person, wherever you live
  - phone calls with people you meet
  - online games with Japanese players: voice chat is low-pressure, high-volume speaking. Harry's biggest speaking leap came from gaming with Japanese friends for hours a day.
  - posting Japanese videos about where you live. It brings in Japanese people nearby who want to meet.
- **People who are keen on foreigners or English** adjust to your level. Very talkative people (like gyaru) are great early on, because they carry the conversation.
- **Watch who you copy:** if you mostly talk to one gender, you'll start sounding like them. Your language parent should be the gender you want to sound like.
- **Early conversations:** bluff, lean on default topics, and say what you know.
- **In groups:** first understand the group's rhythm, then jump in at natural moments.

## Shadowing: don't
- **Dedicated shadowing is a waste of time.** It fixes symptoms, not the missing mental model.
  - The sit-down version (microphone, earphones, matching a speaker one-for-one) is time you could spend immersing.
- **Mimicking isn't shadowing.**
  - **Mimicking (the method):** copying how real people talk when you speak, and repeating lines from your videos that you'd actually say.
  - **Shadowing (not the method):** the sit-down drill of speaking over audio in real time.
- **Record yourself and compare instead.** Harry's analogy: shadowing is like driving blindfolded while someone tells you how to steer.
- **What is good:** casually copying while you listen, and saying a line back a few times out loud when it's something you'd actually say.

## Your language parent
- **One good-enough person first** for your overall feel and rhythm. Later, borrow specific features from other speakers.
- **If nobody fits perfectly,** compromise on topic or age, not on gender. Pick someone you like the sound of, with lots of engaging, unscripted content.
- **Same person as your main creator?** Ideally yes. Your main creator (Stage 2) is who you build your understanding on, and they often become the language parent you copy.
- **What to copy:** fillers, rhythm, reactions, how they connect ideas, and their chunks. Not what they talk about.
- **Accent:** your language parent decides it.
- **Age gap:** only matters if you're copying someone much older.

## Feedback: how to know when you're wrong
- **Most Japanese people will tell you your Japanese is great no matter what,** so you can't rely on them.
- **The main way:** compare yourself against your language parent, by ear and with Claude, and watch how natives react to what you say. An honest close friend who'll correct you is a bonus.
- **The AI speech comparison:**
  1. Record yourself talking alone, unedited, for 20-40 minutes, about the kind of things your speaker talks about.
  2. Pick 3 or more videos of your language parent talking alone (30+ minutes).
  3. Run the **JIL speech comparison** skill in Claude Code (the classroom's Toolkit lesson sets it up). It transcribes both and gives you a **short list of the 5-10 easiest fixes**, each showing how your speaker really says it.
  4. Without Claude Code: give Claude a transcript of each and ask for the 5-10 easiest fixes, quoting your speaker's actual lines.

  Don't ask for every difference. Fix those, practise them for a week or two, record again and repeat. Text can't show sounds or pitch: check those by ear.

## Pitch accent and pronunciation
- **You learn pitch by listening.** The rules only plant the seeds.
  - "The pitch goes up here" is vague and doesn't tell you what it actually sounds like.
  - The Hikaru 500 shows you standard pitch on its words so you know what to listen out for.
  - After that, your ear picks it up from immersion until a word's sound and its pitch are inseparable.
- **Don't obsess.** You never grade pitch, your own sentence cards don't need it, and perfect pitch isn't the goal.
- **Pronunciation:** notice where you sound different from natives (recording yourself makes this obvious) and practise those specific sounds on purpose. Listening builds the ear; practice closes the gap.
- **Accent work has a point of diminishing returns.** A bit of foreigner flavour is fine.

## Kansai-ben
- **A real option, and it's your choice.** Mixing in Kansai-ben (like Hikaru does) can sound natural and fun, and it hides pitch mistakes, so you come across as "not from around here" rather than foreign.
- **Your language parent decides it.** If you copy someone from Osaka, you'll pick it up.
- The course materials stay standard Japanese, because Kansai pitch resources are unreliable.

## Politeness and keigo
- **Why politeness matters:** Japanese changes with the relationship (age, standing, how well you know each other), and people judge you on it.
- **The levels:**
  - polite です/ます is your everyday floor with people you don't know
  - full keigo for first meetings, people senior to you, and work
  - shortened casual forms only once you're close
- **Practise keigo on purpose.** Learning from YouTube leaves you casual-heavy, because most of what you hear is friends chatting. Keigo isn't just different endings: it's the whole sentence structure and how you ask things nicely.
- **Harry's experience:** speaking very politely, even over the top, made strangers, shop staff and contacts like him far more. The better your Japanese sounds, the more politeness people expect from you.

## Sounding natural at a high level
- **Aim for natural details** over textbook correctness or heavy slang.
- **Push past what you can already say** and move words from "I understand it" to "I use it".
- **At an advanced level,** upgrade the patterns you overuse and add more complex vocabulary without sounding like a book. Listening to articulate speakers is what drives this.
