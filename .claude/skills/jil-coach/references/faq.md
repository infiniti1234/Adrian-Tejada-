# What members ask most, answered the way Harry answers now

These are the real questions from the Skool calls and Q&As, grouped by theme. The detail behind each answer is in the topic files.

## Getting started
**Where do I start, and what should my day look like?**
- **Day one:** phone to Japanese, a Japanese-only Instagram (30-60 min), log it in the JIL Tracker, post an intro in the Skool.
- **Then:** kana in a notebook if you can't read it yet (1-2 weeks), then the Hikaru 500 in Anki.
- **A normal day:** Anki reviews then new cards (start at 10), at least an hour of active listening if you can (about 2 hours is ideal), passive listening in every gap, and talking to yourself in your head.

**How long until I'm conversational?** Fairly fluent in about a year at 2+ hours of active listening a day; a high level often takes 2-4 years. Less time just means slower. With a patient partner you can hold a basic one-on-one chat after a few hundred words.

**Is my daily amount enough?** There's no minimum. Even 20 minutes moves you, slowly.
- **The ideal:** about 2 hours of active a day.
- **Busy people:** an hour in 15-30 minute blocks works, plus passive listening in every gap.
- **"I only have one hour."** Harry doesn't believe many people really do. Get up 30 minutes earlier and that's 30 minutes of watching. Look for the 10, 15 and 30 minute pockets through your day, not big 2-hour blocks; they add up.
- **If you truly only have one sit-down hour** (or Anki fills it): use the hour for Anki, and do your listening as passive: the one video you're working through, in your earphones as much as you can all day.
- **Listening isn't optional.** No listening at all is like wanting to be an MMA fighter without training. Less work still gets some results, just less, and below a point it won't happen.

**How do I fit Japanese around work and busy days?** Gaps. Anki on the commute or in a break (20 minutes of a 30-minute break is great), passive listening while you work out, drive or do chores. On a heavy roster, push words before it starts, keep reviews ticking over, and do extra immersion on days off.

**Is it talent? Am I too old?** It's the method: natural sources, volume and feedback. Chunks and context work at any age; younger people just get away with more brute force.

## Kana, kanji and grammar
**Do I need the kanji deck?** Only if you're a true beginner without a Chinese-language background. It's 300 kanji, and its only job is to make kanji stop looking like squiggles. Anyone with some Japanese can skip it. You don't learn readings from it; words come with their kanji in your other cards.

**Should I learn to write kanji?** Not as a beginner. It's an optional extra much later, for people who need handwriting (e.g. working in Japan).

**I've studied Japanese before. Where do I start?** Same path as everyone, faster through the base. Skip kana if you read it and skip the kanji deck. Skim Tae Kim fast for anything new. Go through the Hikaru 500 and delete every card where you know every word and understand the whole sentence. Then make decks with the deck-maker like everyone else; it already counts every Hikaru 500 word and the words in your existing Anki decks as known, and you delete any card that teaches you nothing new.

**How much grammar do I need? Is Tae Kim enough?** Read Tae Kim once, all of it, briefly, until each point roughly makes sense. Then let immersion teach the rest. When you know every word in a sentence and still can't see how it means what it means, ask Claude to break it down.

**How do I handle long clauses before nouns, who-does-what-to-whom, causatives?** Read that Tae Kim section once so you know what to listen for, then pick the patterns up as chunks from your videos. Don't drill them.

**I understand by translating in my head. Is that a problem?** It's normal early on, but don't make it the habit. When you grade cards, ask "does this make sense in my head?", not "can I translate it?". The goal is Japanese straight to meaning.

## Anki and cards
**How many new cards a day? Is 50 realistic?** As many as you can while retention stays around 75-80%. Start at 10; about 20 is normal; 50 is fine if your retention holds (Harry aimed for 50-60 a day for his N1, some days only 20-30). If you're struggling at 20, post your cards in the Skool.

**How do I separate new cards from reviews and get through Anki faster?**
- Set new cards per day to 9999, bulk-add your cards and suspend them, then unsuspend a set of 10 at a time.
- Do your reviews first.
- Speed-run new cards the first time they show: show the answer, read the breakdown, picture the word, press Good, and let the next showing decide. Reviews get graded properly.
- Grade on feel (does it mean something to you?), not by translating.
- Swap out cards that keep failing.
- If retention is well under 75-80%, add fewer new cards so reviews don't snowball.

**How strictly should I grade?** The reading must be right. The meaning just has to make sense in your head, even vaguely. Never grade pitch.

**Why won't some words stick?** Usually the sentence. It's unclear, has too many unknown words, or you don't fully understand it. Swap it for a cleaner sentence with one new word (on a Hikaru 500 card, don't edit it: post it in the Skool instead, since each card builds on the ones before). Adding furigana is a fix for a stubborn card, not a habit. Some cards just keep coming back, even for Harry.

**Do my cards need audio?** No, it's completely optional. If you want it, the sentence audio goes on the back by default (on the front if you mainly want speed in listening and speaking). Use ElevenLabs with your key kept in a file on your computer, never pasted into a chat. Harry never had audio on his own cards.

**Are premade decks okay (Core 6k, frequency decks, other people's decks)?** No. Someone else decided what's common for you, and your words depend on what you watch. Use the starter decks (the Hikaru 500, the optional kanji deck, and the JLPT deck if you're sitting it), then make your own cards from your videos with Claude.

**I already have a premade deck (Core 6k etc.). What do I do with it?** No new cards from it. Keep reviewing it as long as its retention stays above 75%. Below that, suspend it: a lot of premade decks have bad sentences that drag your retention down, and the reviews would run in the hundreds forever. Its words stay on your known-words list either way.

**Where should my new words come from after the Hikaru 500?**
- **First:** every word and chunk you don't know in the video you're working through.
- **Second:** words that pop out at you in immersion, written in one phone note and carded in batches.

**How do I turn a video into a deck with Claude?**
1. Get the video's transcript.
2. Give Claude the transcript plus your known-words list. Ask for sentence cards for every unknown word and chunk: one new word per card, in the video's style, varied, with clear context.
3. Import them into your master deck.

**One master deck or one per show?** One master deck for all your own cards, not one per show or video. The Hikaru 500 stays its own deck, and the kanji deck is separate until you delete it.

**Do I need to learn every word, even rare ones?** Every word in the one video you're working through, yes. Elsewhere, only the ones that pop out at you. Rare bookish words come much later, from dense reading.

**Should I push Anki or immersion right now? Does Anki ever end?** Push hard to about 4,000-5,000 words in your first 3-6 months (counted from day one: about 22 new cards a day gets you 4,000 in 6 months) while still listening daily. After that, slow right down and put the time into listening. Anki then comes back in short bursts when you start a new topic. Harry still does daily reviews.

**When should I go monolingual?** At about 4,000 words. Ask Claude for a short Japanese definition, one layer deep: unknown words inside it get English meanings, not more Japanese definitions. **Which Japanese dictionary?** Claude.

**What happens if I take a break?** Your reviews pile up, and that's fine. When you're back, stop new cards, clear the backlog over a few days, and restart new cards once retention is back around 75-80%. Keep listening during the break if you can.

## Listening and immersion
**I understand almost nothing of native speech. Is that normal?** Completely. Understanding comes in jumps, not a smooth line. Keep the hours up, narrow down to one creator, and let it build.

**Active vs passive: how much, and what if I can't focus?** As much active as you physically can, plus passive every second you can reasonably have earphones in. Nobody holds intense focus for hours in one go, so use stimulating content and switch the moment your focus slips.

**Should I loop the same content or watch broadly?** Both, in different slots. Your one ~15-minute grind video gets learned completely and looped in passive time. Your enjoyable active time is broad: anything that holds your attention.

**Subtitles or raw?** Don't stress. Try not to read burned-in subtitles, but glancing is fine, and early on it helps you spot common structures. To work out whether you're missing words or your ears are the problem, pause and check the subtitles.

**Should I look a word up on the spot or keep going?** Keep going. Only look up words that pop out after you've heard them again and again. Then look them up and write them down.

**When can I watch anime or dramas without subtitles?** Anime and dramas aren't a niche to build on, so there's no milestone for them as such. Once you understand most everyday YouTubers (around 10,000 words), most content makes sense, and by 8,000-10,000 words most immersion does.

**Is reading or listening better?** Listening, for all the time you set aside for immersion. Anki gives you plenty of reading. Dense reading comes at an advanced level for rare words.

**Are learner podcasts, dramas and reality shows okay?** Unscripted is the key. Reality shows and unscripted chat are great. Learner podcasts are false progress; before about 500 words, a Japanese-only Instagram does that job instead. Anime and dramas are fine for fun, but they're not your niche.

**Does anime with English subtitles count?** No. It's leisure: enjoy it as leisure, but it doesn't count as study in any way, and it doesn't go in the tracker.

**Should I keep my Duolingo streak?** No. Drop Duolingo completely and put that time into Anki and listening.

**How do I find good Japanese content?** Ask Claude for Japanese YouTubers in your interests and demographic, or search your interests like you would in English and follow the rabbit hole. For podcasts, use Japanese podcasts and YouTube talk channels.

**What is a niche, and how do I pick and expand it?** Your niche is one creator (or one area) you focus on first, so a limited set of words repeats until you understand it fast.
- **Picking:** someone unscripted you like the sound of, ideally your age and gender. Hikaru is the default.
- **Expanding:** next video, then their channel, then similar creators, then new topics.

**How do I pick a language parent when nobody fits?** Compromise on topic or age. A same-age YouTuber in one niche is enough. Pick someone you like listening to, with lots of unscripted content.

**Does living in Japan help?** It's not needed; Harry never lived there. What matters is making Japanese the language you live in wherever you are.

## Speaking
**When should I start speaking? Will it ruin my accent?** Now. It won't. Talk in your head constantly from day one, and talk to real people as soon as you can.

**How do I find the words when I speak and move vocab from passive to active?** Talk in your head and ask Claude how to say what you can't. Rep it until it comes out easily. Learn chunks, because they're the pieces you actually build sentences from.

**Why can I only make simple sentences?** You can't produce what you don't understand. More input and more chunks lift your output. Push past what you can already say when you talk to yourself.

**Should I wait until I'm sure a sentence is correct?** No. Say it. Practise stretch sentences in your head, use what you've practised in conversation, and notice how natives say it back to you.

**Should I learn set phrases, chunks and fillers?** Chunks, yes: they're as important as grammar. Fillers and reactions come from copying your language parent. Set phrases and proverbs, only lightly.

**How do I find speaking partners? HelloTalk isn't working.**
- **HelloTalk** is still the best central place. Talk to lots of people; the ones keen on foreigners or English adjust to your level.
- **Also:** in person, phone calls, and online games with voice chat.
- **Posting Japanese videos about your area** brings people to you.

**How do I keep up in fast group conversations?** First get comfortable understanding that kind of fast, casual speech through immersion. In the group, catch the rhythm and jump in at natural moments with things you can say confidently.

**How do I know when I'm wrong, or which word natives actually use?** Most natives will say you're great regardless.
- Compare yourself with your language parent, by ear and with Claude (the AI speech comparison: 5-10 easiest fixes).
- Notice how natives say it back to you.
- An honest close friend is a bonus.

**Should I shadow?** No. Record yourself and compare instead.

**I hate my mistakes / feel like a fraud. Does it get better?** Yes. Being able to hear your own mistakes means you're improving. Everyone feels like they're bluffing at some point, Harry included. Keep listening, keep a narrow niche, and trust that complex sentences start coming out naturally.

## Pronunciation, pitch, dialect, politeness
**How do I learn pitch accent?** By ear. The Hikaru 500 shows standard pitch on its words so you know what to listen for; immersion does the rest. Don't obsess, and never grade it.

**How do I fix my pronunciation?** Record yourself, compare with your language parent, find the specific sounds that differ, and practise those on purpose.

**Should I learn Kansai-ben or another dialect?** Don't study it as a subject. Your language parent decides it: copy someone from Kansai and you'll pick it up; copy a standard speaker and you won't. A standard-Kansai mix is a real, fun option, and it hides pitch mistakes. The course materials stay standard.

**Should I learn keigo first?** Polite です/ます is your floor from the start. Full keigo is a Stage 3 focus, or earlier if you're living or working in Japan. Default to polite です/ます with people you don't know, and full keigo for first meetings, seniors and work. YouTube makes you casual-heavy, so practise formal speech on purpose. People notice and like it.

## Progress and plateaus
**How do I find out whether speed, words or grammar is blocking me?** The subtitle test.
- Pause a subtitled video.
- **Lots of unknown words?** It's words.
- **You know them all but your ears missed them?** It's your ear: pause or cut back new cards for a while and put that time into listening.
- **You know the words but can't see how the sentence works?** That's the one time to dig into grammar with Claude.

**When am I ready to move on?**
- **Stage 1 → 2:** the Hikaru 500 is done, you've read Tae Kim once, and the kanji deck is done if you needed it.
- **Stage 2 → 3:** about 4,000 words.
- **Stage 3 → 4:** about 10,000 words, when you understand most everyday YouTubers who aren't on specialist topics.

**How do I get near-native?** Better speakers, complex topics, a language parent, constant feedback, and lots of real conversation. Dense reading comes in for the rare words. There's no finish line; you keep picking new niches.
